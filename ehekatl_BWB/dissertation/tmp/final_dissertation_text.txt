Abstract


    This thesis presents the design and control of EHEKATL, a blended-wing-body
(BWB) aircraft research platform. The work addresses the aerodynamic and geometric
design of the vehicle, nonlinear dynamic modelling, nonlinear controller design, numer-
ical simulation, and controller-in-the-loop evaluation in the X-Plane flight-simulation
environment.
    The EHEKATL configuration was developed using XFLR5 to estimate aerodynamic
characteristics relevant to stability and control. The selected aerofoils were then imple-
mented in Airfoil Maker, and a complete aircraft model was constructed in Plane Maker,
including its geometry, aerodynamic properties, mass and balance, onboard systems,
and performance characteristics. A six-degree-of-freedom nonlinear dynamic model was
subsequently derived from Newtonian mechanics and separated into longitudinal and
lateral-directional subsystems for control design and analysis.
    Backstepping and adaptive-backstepping control laws were developed to address
the nonlinear aircraft dynamics and uncertainty in parameters that may be unknown
or vary with the operating condition. Their behaviour was first evaluated through
numerical simulations. Controller-in-the-loop simulations were then conducted in X-
Plane for representative flight tasks.
    The reported simulations indicate that the backstepping controller can stabilise
the EHEKATL model in both the longitudinal and lateral-directional axes under the
evaluated conditions. These results establish a basis for reproducible validation over
a wider flight envelope, independent actuation of distributed control surfaces, optimal
control allocation, and subsequent physical testing of the EHEKATL research platform.

Introduction


1.1     Context of the problem
The global commercial market forecast of different companies, such as Airbus, Boeing
and Commercial Aircraft Corporation of China (COMAC), indicates robust aviation
growth at an average annual rate of 4.5% over the next 20 years, (4).
    On the other hand, the current consensus indicates that aviation contributes a 2-3%
of the total carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions produced by anthropogenic emissions, (4).
However, the total effect on global warming is probably closer to a 3-5% when we talk
about the effects of nitrogen oxides (NOx) from combustion. If everything goes as it
has done so far, the impact of aviation emissions on climate change will grow as fast as
commercial flights grow. (4).
    According to the European Aviation Vision projection, the fuel efficiency of new
aviation transports is expected to improve by 2050, while CO2 and NOx emissions will
have to be reduced by 75% and 90%, respectively, (1). This ambitious target needs
improvements in the technologies applied to current transport aircraft (3). One of the
keys to reducing polluting emissions is through the design of more efficient air vehicles,
especially aircraft that reduce aerodynamic drag, their structural weight as well as
improving the efficiency of their engines.
    The conventional tube-wing configuration1 has been optimized to the limit of its
architecture, and although innovative technologies have made turbofan engines (such as
the adoption of a higher by-pass ratio) and lighter and more resistant wing and fuselage
structures (thanks to composite materials) it is very difficult that this configuration can
meet the Europe Vision mentioned by 2050 with the objective of operating a neutral
aviation of polluting emissions.

1.2       Background and motivation
The main challenge with the BWB aircraft model is in the stabilization and control
area. Designing a wing-integrated by aerodynamic optimization without stability con-
siderations would bring with it a degraded practice. Thus, an optimal multidisciplinary
design is needed where in addition to the principles of aerodynamic design (maximizing
lifting and minimizing drag) moment coefficients that ensure stability and control are
considered.
     BWB unlike TAW, lacks impeneage, where horizontal and vertical stabilizers are
usually located with aerodynamic control surfaces2 This absence reassigns new func-
tions to the ACS, for example, steering rudders for the yawing moment can be imple-
mented on the fins at the wing tips or in vertical stabilizers in the center of the body.
However, if both stabilizers are adopted, aerodynamic efficiency could be compromised.
Thus, correctly dimensioning the use of vertical stabilizers is essential for the synergy
between aerodynamic efficiency and the assurance of stability and control.
     In BWB and FW the lifts for the pitching are located on the trailing edge, where
we also have the spoilers for the time of plowing. This generates that the arm moment
of the lifts is shorter than in a TAW model and comes in the possibility of lacking
the necessary power at low speeds or adverse conditions, which would put at risk
the passengers and crew, or compromising the mission and integrity of the aircraft
in the case of an unmanned BWB vehicle. To compensate for the short moment of
pitching there is a greater range of deflection and a greater number of control inputs.
A conventional TAW model normally has a pair of lifts and wingers. Instead in a BWB
we could have from a pair to two or three pairs of each.

2.2         Origin of BWB
According to Bruce I. Larrimer in (10)In the summer of 1988, Dennis Bushell of NASA
invited through letters representatives of McDonnell Douglas, aeronautical engineers
and aerodynamics to the NASA Langley Research Center (LaRC), to answer and resolve
the following question: Is there room for large-capacity and long-range air transport that
generates an aerodynamic renaissance?. Bushnell mainly questioned the evolutionary
step in the design of transport aircraft, highlighting that revolutionary development
such as Boeing 707 and Douglas DC 8, the first generation of arrow-wing jets that
revolutionized international air trade, had been successful by designs that follow a
cautious, incremental and evolutionary pattern line. In the spring of 1989, nearly
two dozen government and industry leaders, along with aeronautical academics and
engineers and aerodynamics, gathered at NASA’s LaRC to discuss possible new aircraft
configurations. The Reference Matrix1 according to the TAW configuration was named
Transport of Synergistic Technology Transport or STT n in English with the aim of
unifying the philosophies of evolution between the models of aircraft DC-10 and MD-
11. Robert H.Liebeck together with his colleagues from McDonnell Douglas presented
the aircraft of wing-integrated as a proposal of evolution and revolution, see Figure 2.8.
The characteristics of the flight mission for the comparative analysis between STT and
the first BWB concept were an operating range of 8,500 nautical miles with a capacity
for 368 passengers travelling at a cruise speed of 0.85 Mach. The results showed a

3.2.1    Selection of Alalar Profiles in XFLR5
The aerodynamic profile or wing profile is the geometry of the cross section in a lateral
view of the supporting surface or wing. reflex are non-symmetric profiles that have a
medium comba line with double slight curvature in the form of S, which is why they
are also known as ”S” profiles, normally used in wings-flying and integrated wings.
(25), the most significant feature of the reflex profiles is that they are profiles with




                                            30
                                                                 3.2 Aerodynamic design


positive stability, that is to say their moment coefficient is always positive except and
very occasionally at very high attack angles, this results in that the wing with this type
of profile flies in balance without the need for horizontal stabilizers, so its widespread
use is in delta type wings, wing-flyers and integrated wings. Under these conditions
the centre of gravity of the model shall be located in the direction of the resulting
aerodynamics that always passes through the pressure centre that is determined by the
moment coefficient and supporting coefficient. The theoretical point of the wing where
the lift is considered applied is called a centre of pressure. For theoretical purposes,
although the pressure acts on the whole profile, it is considered that all the lift is
exerted on a point in the line of the rope (resulting). The position of the pressure
centre is usually given in % of the wing rope from the edge of attack. In the case of
self-stable profiles it places the center point between 20 and 25 % of the aerodynamic
mid-string. If an external disturbance alters the position of the wing, causing this
spike, its center of pressure, to move forward away from the center of gravity, which
immediately causes a moment of encabritation (above) that tends to the wing regain
its initial balance. The main drawback of these types of profiles is that they have
relatively low lift coefficients while their drag coefficients are high. Visually, aircraft
with these types of profiles appear to have the trailing edge and the ailerons slightly
oriented upwards. This geometry is responsible for generating stability at the moment
of pitching. If we used a symmetrical profile instead of a profile reflex In the design
of a wing-integrated we would need a wide bending capacity of the elevators and a
complex control system for a stable flight, which generates a constant consumption of
energy given the continuous deflection of the effectors, and therefore would decrease
the aerodynamic performance of the aircraft.

Dynamic Modeling

Once the aerodynamic design is completed, this chapter presents the dynamic model
of EHEKATL BWB. Since aircraft are considered complex dynamic systems during
their flight operation and are not stable by themselves, they need a control system
to fly in the desired direction and compensate for disturbances. For the dynamic
modeling of EHEKATL BWB we will consider a rigid body of six degrees of freedom;
three degrees of displacement (towards, to one side and downwards) and three degrees
of rotation (to top, to roll and yaw). Thus, we will have six differential equations,
based on Newtonian laws, that will govern the movement of the aircraft. For the
development of the dynamic model EHEKATL BWB is proposed with two engines,
eight aerodynamic control surfaces on the trailing edge and two directional rudders on
the vertical stabilizers. In A.2, the modeling for EHEKATL BWB TVC is proposed
considering a nozzle with deflection for generation of pitching and yawing moments.

Design of Controllers

The controller design is a discipline within aerospace engineering that focuses on the
development of control systems to stabilize and control the movement of aerial vehicles,
space vehicles and rockets. A flight controller is an electronic system that uses accel-
eration, speed and position sensors to determine the trajectory of both manned and
unmanned vehicles. The controller generates a reference command (may be manual,
issued by a pilot; or automatic, issued by a computer) and in response to this reference
command (also known as a track signal or desired trajectory) act the vehicle’s control
inputs to achieve or reach the desired state from a previous state or initial condition.In
the case of EHEKATL BWB, primary control inputs (elevators, spoilers, rudders and
engines) were established to control their movement. The main objective of the flight
controller is to ensure that the aircraft flies in a stable and predictable manner, re-
sponding to the pilot’s commands or following a specific trajectory autonomously.
    The flight controllers are structured into two parts: the measurement system and
the control system. The measurement system uses sensors to measure aircraft variables
such as speed, altitude, attitude and orientation. These data are processed in the
control system that uses algorithms to calculate the commands needed to keep the
aircraft in its desired path. These commands are sent to actuators who are responsible
for moving control effectors, such as engines and aerodynamic control surfaces.
    There are two types of flight controllers: linear and non-linear. Linear controllers
are control systems that use linear equations. These controllers are simple and easy
to implement, but are restricted to an operating range and may not be able to handle
certain disturbances outside of their equilibrium point.
    On the other hand, non-linear controllers try to compensate for highly non-linear
dynamics and their objective is to compensate for this dynamic by means of robust
and model-free controllers, especially in aircraft which are subject to various types of
disturbances or states that require a higher degree of freedom and manoeuvrability.
The choice between a linear or non-linear controller depends on the specific application
and characteristics of the vehicle. In short, the design of flight controllers is essential
to ensure that an aircraft flies ensuring certain operating conditions and stability.
    In this chapter, the non-linear flight controllers Backstepping and Adaptive Back-

5.1     Backstepping
One method introduced and used to explore new directions in systems control design
with non-linear dynamics is Backstepping. Backstepping controllers synthesize a com-
plex non-linear dynamic system (aircraft) into interconnected subsystems. Therefore,
the aircraft dynamic system is divided into a series of subsystems. The objective of con-
trol with Backstepping is to achieve by cascades the overall control of these subsystems,
starting with the last subsystem. The output of the controller becomes the input of the
next subsystem, and the continuous process until reaching the first subsystem. Each
controller subsystem is designed to stabilize and track the desired path of that sub-
system. The Backstepping controller is designed to stabilize and control the aircraft’s
dynamics. We can systematically build controllers that handle the complex dynamics
and non-linearities of the aircraft’s movement, in addition to ensuring stability, (24).

The term Adaptable, as far as control is concerned, has the quality of adjusting param-
eters and/or control actions in order to deal with changes, uncertainties or variations
in the dynamic system. In aircraft dynamics, aerodynamic coefficients are complex
to obtain accurately, and uncertainties and variations can occur over time due to fac-
tors such as external disturbances, changes in system parameters and environmental
conditions.

the complex dynamics of the system where accurate knowledge of system dynamics can
be a challenge, in addition to the changes they experience during flight operation.

5.4     Discussion
The backstepping-based control approach is a technique commonly used for the design
of controllers in non-linear dynamic systems. The application of backstepping in the
context of the EHEKATL BWB aircraft provides a sound methodology for addressing
the stabilization and tracking of flight paths, considering the non-linearities of the
system.
     The introduction of adaptability in controller design through the adaptive backstep-
ping approach represents an important extension. The ability to adapt the controller to
changes in system parameters or variable flight conditions is essential to ensure optimal
performance in various operating situations.
     Benchmarking designed controllers is crucial to determine their effectiveness and
efficiency in the specific context of EHEKATL BWB. Performance indicators provide
quantitative metrics to assess the stability, accuracy of tracking and transient response
of controllers under different flight scenarios.

Conclusions and perspectives

The present thesis work represents an integral effort in the design and control of the
EHEKATL BWB aircraft. Throughout this research, a number of fundamental stages
were carried out that addressed from aerodynamic design to the implementation of
advanced controllers, culminating in flight simulations in the X-PLANE environment.
    During the design phase, a combination of specialized tools, such as XFLR5, Airoil
Maker and Plane Maker, was used to design and evaluate the aerodynamic profile
of the EHEKATL BWB aircraft. This process provided a detailed understanding of
the pressure distribution along the wing surface, as well as the supporting and drag
coefficients. The resulting optimization allowed outstanding aerodynamic efficiency,
fundamental to flight performance. XFLR5 released a performance for EHEKATL
BWB between 15 and 20 with respect to the polar. CL /CD as a function of α. In X-
PLANE performance was achieved between 15 at the lowest speeds (Mach 0.04) and 27
at the highest speeds (Mach 0.085). A great advantage of our methodology is that all
the software is open source and free, except for X-PLANE that to release the simulation
time from fifteen minutes to unlimited and all the scenarios you have to buy the full
version, however, all the work shown here is developed with the free version.
    The development of the six-degree dynamic model of freedom represented a critical
milestone in the understanding of the aircraft’s behaviour. By considering the longi-

A.1     Simulations with XFLR5
XFLR5 is an open source software widely used for aerodynamic analysis and aircraft de-
sign. It was developed mainly by Mark Drela, a leading aerospace engineer and professor
at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT). His experience in aerodynamics
and aircraft design was fundamental to the creation of this tool. The development of
XFLR5 began in the 1990s, which means it has been in development and evolution for
several decades. This has allowed the tool to mature and become a reliable option for
aircraft engineers and designers. XFLR5 is distributed under an open source license,
which means that the software is free and its source code is available for anyone to
examine, modify and distribute. This has fostered an active community of users and
developers who contribute to its continuous improvement. It specializes in aerodynamic
analysis of aircraft and gliders. It offers key features such as aerodynamic coefficient
calculation, polar curve generation, flight simulation, aerodynamic profile optimization
and more.

analysis.

    Theory of the line of support It is a simplified model that is used to un-
     derstand and analyze the aerodynamics of a wing, especially in the context of
     finite wings (wings with a limited wingspan rather than infinite wings).You can
     model the aerodynamics of a wing by dividing it into segments, each treated
     as a separate aerodynamic profile.These segments are connected along the wing’s
     wingspan, forming a continuous line or curve known as a ”support line.”Each seg-
     ment generates individual support based on its local angle of attack and the char-
     acteristics of the aerodynamic profile.The Sustainability Line Theory in XFLR5
     allows you to approximate the sustained and induced resistance of a finite wing.It
     is a valuable tool to analyze the performance of aircraft wings in the real world.

    Horseshoe vortexes A horseshoe vortex is a complex flow pattern that occurs
     near the tips of the wings of an aircraft or other supporting bodies. When an
     aircraft generates support, it creates a pressure difference between the top and
     bottom surfaces of its wings. This pressure difference induces air flow from the
     high pressure region below the wing towards the low pressure region above. Near
     the tips of the wings, this flow is rolled up and forms a vortex pattern that re-
     sembles a horseshoe. This horseshoe vortex is a significant contributor to induced
     resistance. Understanding the presence and behavior of the horseshoe vortex is
     crucial for aircraft design and aerodynamic optimization. Engineers seek to min-
     imize the intensity of these vortexes to reduce resistance and improve efficiency.

    Invalid Vortex XFLR5 does not directly model annular vortexes, as they are
     typically associated with stele turbulence and aircraft interaction in real-world
     scenarios. However, the software can provide information about flow behavior
     near the wing tips and leak edges that can contribute to the formation of annular
     vortexes. Although XFLR5 does not explicitly simulate annular vortexes, it can
     help you understand the flow patterns and conditions that could lead to their
     formation. This knowledge can guide you in the design of wings and aircraft to
     minimize the impact of stele turbulence.

    Paneles 3D In the context of XFLR5 and the aerodynamic analysis of aircraft,
     the ”3D panels” refer to a numerical technique that breaks down the wing surface
     and aircraft into a series of two-dimensional elements (panels) representing parts
     of the surface. These 3D panels can be considered as flat surfaces that roughly
     follow the shape of the wing and aircraft in three dimensions. Each 3D panel
     has associated aerodynamic properties, such as angle of attack, curvature, space
     position and aerodynamic coefficients. XFLR5 uses this 3D panel decomposition

following: limitaciones in the use of XFLR5: 1) Potential Flow Models: XFLR5 is
based on potential flow models, which means that it does not solve the complete Navier-
Stokes equations, but is based on simplifications. This makes it suitable for analysis of
aerodynamic wings and profiles at subsonic and transonic speeds, but is not suitable for
analysis of supersonic or hypersonic flows. 2) Small Disturbation Approach: XFLR5
assumes that the aircraft-induced disturbances in the flow are small, which is valid for
most conventional aeronautical applications. However, it is not suitable for the analy-
sis of high speed or high manoeuvrability aircraft, such as supersonic combat aircraft.
3) Simplifications in Geometry: The software uses a series of geometrical simplifica-
tions, such as two-dimensional wing profiles and straight wing models. This may limit
your ability to model complex geometries or variable wing effects. 4) Limitations on
Operation Conditions: XFLR5 is more suitable for analysis of subsonic and transonic
operating conditions. It is not the appropriate tool for analysis of hypersonic aircraft
or rockets. 5) Sensitivity to Entry Data: The accuracy of simulations in XLR5 can
depend to a large extent on the quality of input data, such as aerodynamic profiles and
flow properties. Results may vary if data is not accurate or if important information is
lacking. 6) Limited Analysis Capabilities: Although it is useful for aerodynamic wing
and profile analysis, XFLR5 does not have the most advanced analysis capabilities that
offer complete Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) tools. It cannot capture complex
three-dimensional flow effects, advanced stability and control effects or complex air-to-
air interactions. 7) It is not a Final Design Tool: XFLR5 is useful for conceptual design
and preliminary analysis, but it is not designed for detailed aircraft design and analysis
at the final engineering level. It is a valuable tool for the initial design phase, but
designers often resort to more specialized tools for further analysis and validation. 8)
Accuracy in viscous trawling: XFLR5 is mainly based on methods of potential analysis
and is therefore more suitable for calculating support and induced trawling (associated
with support). However, it has limitations in the precise calculation of viscous trawling,
which is the drag related to the friction of viscous flow on the surface of a wing. XFLR5
uses a simplified viscous trawl model that assumes that the flow is incomprehensible
and that the boundary layer is thin. This can lead to inaccuracies in the calculation of
drag in certain cases, especially in configurations where the boundary layer is signifi-
cant or in high speed aerodynamic profiles.It is designed primarily for two-dimensional
analysis and, although it can handle 3D configurations, it is not as accurate for highly

A.7        Linear Quadratic Regulator

The Linear Quadratic Regulatory Control1 is an advanced control technique used in
linear dynamic systems. Your goal is to design a controller that minimizes a quadratic
cost function. The LQR control is based on two key aspects: the formulation of a
quadratic cost function that quantifies the desired performance of the system and the
resolution of the Riccati equation to obtain the driver gains that minimize this cost
function. Recalling the dynamic model chapter, for the LQR controller we use the
EHEKATL BWB system in state space given the shape ẋ = Ax + Bu, where x is the
vector of states and u is the vector of control inputs.
    The aim is to minimize a quadratic cost function defined as:
                                   Z ∞
                               J=       (xT Qx + uT Ru)dt                      (A.183)
                                            0

where Q and R are the weighting matrices that determine the relative importance of
states and control inputs in the total cost.
    The Riccati equation in the LQR controller is a matrix equation that is used to find
the optimal winning matrix K The equation is as follows:

                              AT P + P A − P BR−1 B T P + Q = 0                     (A.184)
   1
       Linear Quadratic Regulator, or LQR




                                                165
A. CODE/MANUALS/PUBLICATIONS


where A is the matrix of states of the system, B is the input matrix of the system, R
is the weighting matrix of the control entries, Q is the weighting matrix of the states
and P It’s the solution matrix we’re looking for.
    To apply the LQR Control, you must first have the system model in the form of
state space with the matrices A and B defined. Subsequently, Q and R weighting
matrices based on control objectives are chosen. Choosing these matrices will affect
controller performance. The Riccati equation is a quadratic equation in P , and can be
solved in various ways depending on the nature of the matrices involved. The solution
can be analytical or numerical. In practice, it is common to use computational tools to
find the numerical solution to the Riccati equation. The library is used control.matlab
for Python to estimate P . The matrix P contains information on how system states
and control inputs are related to minimize quadratic cost function. The solution of the
Riccati equation provides optimal gains for the LQR controller, which means that the
resulting controller will minimize the quadratic cost function. It is important to note
that the Riccati equation can have multiple solutions. In practice, the solution will
depend on the specific objectives of the controller design.
